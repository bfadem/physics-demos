Exclusive OR

One function, five ways to build it — and the reason a single 14-pin package of NAND gates has exactly enough gates to do the job with nothing left over.

The function

A difference detector

Plain OR asks: is at least one input high? Exclusive OR asks a sharper question: are the two inputs different? It goes high when exactly one input is high, and low when the inputs agree — whether they agree on 0 or on 1.

That reading is worth holding onto, because it explains almost every place XOR shows up later. A parity checker is asking whether a bit pattern differs from even. A subtractor is asking where two numbers disagree. A comparator is asking whether two words match. All of them are difference detectors.

The usual symbol is ⊕, so the function is written A ⊕ B. In the C-style notation used on this page, & is AND, | is OR, and ~ is NOT.

Why it isn't a primitive

AND, OR, and NOT are the three operations Boolean algebra is built from. XOR is not one of them — it is a function you assemble. Every construction below is an answer to the same question: which arrangement of the three primitives reproduces that column of outputs?

Construction one · from behaviour

At least one, but not both

Start from the description rather than the table. "At least one input is high" is A | B. "Not both are high" is ~(A & B). XOR wants both conditions at once, so AND them together:

X = ( ~( A & B ) ) & ( A | B )   →   ( ~( 0 & 0 ) ) & ( 0 | 0 ) = ( 1 ) & ( 0 ) = 0

Verifying it row by row is the whole proof. The AND column and the OR column are never both favourable except in the two middle rows:

A useful accident

The intermediate signal A & B in this circuit is exactly the carry-out of a half adder. Build XOR this way and the carry bit is already sitting on a node, free. That is not a coincidence — a half adder is an XOR and an AND sharing the same two inputs, and this construction happens to share the AND rather than duplicating it.

Construction two · from the table

Sum of products

The second route ignores any clever reading of the function and works mechanically from the truth table. Find every row whose output is 1. Write one product term per row — a term that is true for that row and false everywhere else. Then OR the terms together.

Row A=0, B=1

True only when A is low and B is high: (~A) & B.

Row A=1, B=0

True only when A is high and B is low: A & (~B).

X = ( ~A & B ) | ( A & ~B )   →   ( 1 & 0 ) | ( 0 & 1 ) = 0 | 0 = 0

These single-row terms are called minterms, and the recipe is completely general: any truth table you can write down, with any number of inputs, converts to a sum-of-products expression this way. That generality is the real payoff. The factored form above required an insight about what XOR means; this one required only the ability to read a table.

Why the K-map can't help

The usual next move after writing a sum of products is to minimise it with a Karnaugh map, grouping adjacent 1s. XOR is the standard example where that fails. Its two 1s sit on the diagonal, and diagonal cells are not adjacent — they differ in both variables, not one. No grouping is possible, so the canonical form is already minimal as a sum of products.

Two-variable K-map for A ⊕ B
B=0B=1
A=001
A=110

The lesson generalises. Any function whose 1s form a checkerboard resists sum-of-products minimisation, and XOR-heavy logic — parity trees, adders, CRC generators — is exactly where K-maps stop being the right tool. The gain has to come from factoring instead, which is what the first construction did.

Small observation

The two minterms are mutually exclusive — never both 1 at the same time. So the OR joining them could be replaced by an XOR without changing the output. True, but circular if you are trying to build XOR in the first place. It matters in a different setting: when terms are provably disjoint, a designer is free to use whichever combining gate is cheaper in the target technology.

Reconciling the two

The same circuit, twice

Two expressions that produce identical truth tables are the same Boolean function, so the row-by-row checks above already prove equivalence. But the algebra is short, and it shows how one becomes the other rather than merely that it does.

X = ~(A & B) & (A | B)
  = (~A | ~B) & (A | B)  De Morgan on the first factor
  = (~A & A) | (~A & B) | (~B & A) | (~B & B)  expand
  = 0 | (~A & B) | (A & ~B) | 0  complement law
  = (~A & B) | (A & ~B)

Two of the four expanded terms die immediately, because a variable ANDed with its own complement is always 0. What survives is precisely the sum of products. So the factored form is the sum of products with the common structure pulled back out — the two are related the way x² − y² and (x+y)(x−y) are related in ordinary algebra.

"Depth" is the longest chain of gates a signal passes through, and it sets the propagation delay. Both three-gate-deep versions settle in about the same time; the NAND version wins on parts, not on speed.

Construction three · one gate type

Down to four NANDs

The factored form is already most of the way to a NAND-only circuit, because its first operation is a NAND. The following four steps carry it the rest of the way. The order matters — each line is the input to the next.

  1. Name the NAND you already have

    Let N = ~(A & B). That is one NAND gate, taking the two inputs directly. The expression becomes X = N & (A | B).

    N = ~( 0 & 0 ) = 1

  2. Distribute N across the OR

    AND distributes over OR exactly as multiplication distributes over addition:

    X = N & (A | B) = (N & A) | (N & B)

    This deliberately makes the expression longer. The point is that both new terms are ANDs, and ANDs are one inversion away from NANDs.

  3. Convert the OR with De Morgan

    Using P | Q = ~( ~P & ~Q ) with P = N&A and Q = N&B:

    X = ~( ~(N & A) & ~(N & B) )

    Every operation left in the expression is a NAND. There is nothing further to convert.

  4. Read it as hardware

    Three ~( … & … ) patterns are visible, plus the one hiding inside N:

    N = ~(A & B) = 1  ·  P = ~(N & A) = 1  ·  Q = ~(N & B) = 1  ·  X = ~(P & Q) = 0

    Four NAND gates, and the wiring falls out of the four definitions with no further thought.

The bottom row is the one worth checking by hand, because it is where the circuit does its least obvious work. With both inputs high, N goes low. A low input to a NAND forces its output high regardless of the other input, so P and Q are both driven high, and gate 4 pulls X low. Both-high gives low, as XOR requires.

Timing

Signals reach gate 4 by two different routes. A travels through gate 2 in one gate delay, but it also travels through gate 1 and then gate 2, taking two. The longest path is three gates deep, so the output is not trustworthy until three propagation delays after an input changes. For 74LS parts that is roughly 30 ns; a dedicated 74LS86 XOR settles in about a third of that. Check the datasheet for the family actually on the bench — delays vary by an order of magnitude between LS, HC, and HCT.

In the interval before things settle, the output can briefly show a wrong value — a glitch, or static hazard. It is harmless when the output feeds a clocked flip-flop that samples later, and a genuine bug when it feeds something asynchronous. Worth putting on a scope.

On the bench

One 7400, no leftovers

The 7400 is a quad two-input NAND: four independent gates in a 14-pin DIP. The XOR needs exactly four. That tidiness is why this circuit turns up in every introductory lab.

The four jumpers

Everything beyond power, inputs, and output is four short wires:

3 → 5NN into gate 2

6 → 12Pgate 2 output into gate 4
8 → 13Qgate 3 output into gate 4

Input A goes to pins 1 and 4; input B goes to pins 2 and 10. Power on pin 14, ground on pin 7, output read at pin 11.

Things that go wrong

  • Floating inputs. An unconnected TTL input floats high and picks up noise. Tie every input to a defined level through a switch and pull-down, never leave one dangling.
  • Missing decoupling. A 0.1 µF ceramic across pins 14 and 7, right at the package, prevents switching transients from upsetting the other gates.
  • Chip in backwards. The notch marks the pin-1 end. Reversed power usually kills the part immediately and audibly.
  • Reading the wrong output. Pin 11 is X. Pins 3, 6, and 8 are intermediate nodes — useful for probing, wrong for grading.

A good lab exercise

Wire the four-NAND XOR, verify all four rows with a logic probe or LED, then drop a 7486 quad XOR into the adjacent rows of the breadboard and confirm the two agree row for row. Students have then reproduced a real commercial part from primitives, and can see on a scope that the discrete version is measurably slower — the first time propagation delay stops being an abstraction.

Why this always works

NAND is universal

The reduction above was not luck. NAND can express all three Boolean primitives, so it can express anything built from them — every combinational function, without exception. The proof is three lines:

PrimitiveFrom NANDGates
NOT A~(A & A)1
A AND B~( ~(A&B) & ~(A&B) )2
A OR B~( ~(A&A) & ~(B&B) )3

Tying both inputs of a NAND together turns it into an inverter, and everything else follows. NOR is universal for the same reason, by the mirror-image argument. These are the only two two-input gates with the property — which is why silicon libraries are built around them and why a NAND-only redesign is always available as a fallback.

Note the cost, though. Mechanically substituting NAND equivalents into the four-gate factored circuit would give 1 + 3 + 2 = plus the inverter — considerably more than four. The four-gate result came from algebraic restructuring, not substitution. Universality guarantees a NAND-only circuit exists; it says nothing about it being small.

Properties

XOR as arithmetic

XOR behaves like addition modulo 2, and that identity is the reason it is so useful. Every property below is a restatement of "add the bits and throw away the carry."

IdentityMeaning
A ⊕ 0 = A0 is the identity element
A ⊕ 1 = ~AXOR with 1 is a controllable inverter
A ⊕ A = 0every element is its own inverse
A ⊕ B = B ⊕ Acommutative
(A ⊕ B) ⊕ C = A ⊕ (B ⊕ C)associative — so multi-input XOR is unambiguous
A ⊕ B ⊕ B = Aapplying the same key twice restores the original

The second line is the workhorse. An XOR gate with one input tied to a control signal is a programmable inverter: pass the data through when control is 0, invert it when control is 1. Adders use exactly this to switch between addition and subtraction, since subtracting is adding the two's complement, and forming the two's complement means inverting.

The last line is why XOR appears in cryptography. Encryption and decryption are the same operation with the same key, which makes the hardware trivially symmetric. With a truly random key used once, that is a one-time pad — provably unbreakable, and impractical because the key must be as long as the message.

Associativity matters more than it looks. Because grouping does not change the answer, an n-input XOR can be built as a balanced tree of two-input gates rather than a chain, cutting the depth from n−1 to log₂n. For a 32-bit parity tree that is the difference between 31 gate delays and 5.

Where it turns up

Difference detection in the wild

Half and full adders

The sum bit of a half adder is A ⊕ B and the carry is A & B. A full adder is two half adders plus an OR. Every binary adder ever fabricated has XOR gates on its critical path.

Parity

XOR a whole word together and the result is 1 exactly when an odd number of bits are set. That single bit, transmitted alongside the data, catches any single-bit error. RAID 5 uses the same idea across whole disks — the parity drive is the XOR of the others, so any one lost drive can be reconstructed.

Comparators

Two words are equal when every bitwise XOR is 0, so an equality test is a row of XORs feeding a NOR.

Gray code

Converting binary to Gray code is G[i] = B[i] ⊕ B[i+1] — one XOR per bit. Gray code changes only one bit per step, which is why rotary encoders use it: no intermediate wrong reading during a transition.

Pseudorandom sequences

A linear-feedback shift register XORs selected taps back into its input. With the right taps it cycles through every non-zero state before repeating, giving cheap deterministic noise for BER testing and spread spectrum.

Controlled-NOT

The quantum CNOT gate flips its target qubit when the control is 1 — on basis states, exactly XOR. Unlike the classical gate it must be reversible, which is why it keeps both inputs rather than collapsing two wires into one.

The XOR swap

Three XORs exchange two registers with no temporary: a ^= b; b ^= a; a ^= b;. It works because of self-inverse and associativity. It is also a bad idea in real code — it breaks when both operands are the same variable, and modern compilers produce faster ordinary swaps. Worth knowing as an illustration of the algebra, not as a technique.

Below the gate level

What it costs in transistors

Gate counting is the right abstraction for breadboard work but the wrong one for silicon. In static CMOS a two-input NAND takes four transistors, so the four-NAND XOR costs sixteen — respectable, but not what a chip designer would use.

Custom cells do better by abandoning the gate abstraction entirely. A transmission-gate XOR steers one input through pass transistors under control of the other, and lands around six to eight transistors including the inverter it needs for the complement. The saving is real but it comes with weaker output drive and worse behaviour when cascaded, so cell libraries usually offer several XOR variants at different sizes and let the synthesis tool choose.

The general point is worth carrying forward: minimal gate count and minimal area are different objectives, and neither automatically gives minimal delay or minimal power. The four-NAND circuit is optimal for the question "how few 7400-style gates?" and unremarkable for any of the others.

Exercises

  • Derive the five-gate NOR-only XOR by the same method used in section 5, starting from (A|B) & ~(A&B) and pushing everything through De Morgan the other way.
  • Show that XNOR — the complement of XOR, high when inputs agree — needs only one extra inverter, or can be had by rearranging the same four NANDs.
  • Write the three-input XOR truth table. Confirm it is high for odd numbers of 1s, then build it from two two-input XORs and explain why associativity permits this.
  • The circuit in section 5 has a fan-out of two on node N. Redraw it so that no node drives more than one gate, and count how many gates that costs.
  • Take the K-map argument further: sketch a three-variable map for A ⊕ B ⊕ C and confirm that no two 1s are adjacent, so all four minterms survive minimisation.
  • On a scope, trigger on a rising edge of A with B held high and measure the delay to X. Compare against the sum of the datasheet's typical values for three gates.
